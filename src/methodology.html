Part I

Methods statement

The Syntoria Institute · Method version 1.2 · 6 October 2026

1

Purpose and scope

#

This document describes the methodology under which The Syntoria Institute produces, verifies, and publishes coaching case studies. Every published case study is produced under this protocol, without exception. The protocol is published to allow external scrutiny: readers, participants, and reviewers can assess any published story against the method stated here. Changes to the method are versioned and dated.

2

Commissioning and conflict of interest

#

Case studies are commissioned by the coach whose engagement is documented. The Syntoria Institute is therefore not a neutral research body and does not present itself as one; it is an independent producer operating under a fixed, published protocol.

Independence is protected structurally rather than asserted:

  1. The engagement fee is fixed and identical regardless of the content of the client's account.
  2. The commissioning coach may flag factual errors and confidentiality issues, but cannot alter the meaning of the account.
  3. A story that does not meet the publication criteria (section 8) is declined, not revised toward publication.
  4. Final approval of every published sentence rests with the client — not with the coach, and not with the Institute.

No compensation, discount, or other item of value is ever conditioned on a favorable account (per FTC 16 CFR Parts 255 and 465).

3

Case selection

#

Each case is a deliberately selected success case: the coach nominates an engagement, typically among their strongest. This selection is disclosed in every published story. Following the Success Case Method (Brinkerhoff, 2003), the purpose of a selected success case is to document what an intervention can achieve at its best under verifiable conditions — not what the average client experiences. No inference about typical outcomes is made or implied.

Eligibility requires that the client agrees to be interviewed independently and that the engagement is recent enough for reliable recall. Engagements may be completed or ongoing; where an engagement is ongoing, a named and dated anchor point is fixed in advance and used as the reference for all retrospective ratings (section 5). Deviations are recorded in the case file.

4

Procedure

#

4.1Impact model

Before any client contact, the engagement's goals as agreed at the time, the expected chain of change, and the engagement facts (dates, session count, format) are recorded with the coach. This is done through a standardized written pre-interview form followed by a working session, so that the record is the coach's own considered account rather than a live improvisation. The interview tests this model; the model is not shown to the client beforehand.

The coach is also asked to predict what the client will say changed. These predictions are quarantined: they are never placed in the interviewer's materials and are compared with the client's answers only after the interview, in analysis. An interviewer who knows the expected answer can steer toward it without intending to.

4.2Invitation

Clients are invited with a standardized email, sent by their own coach. Its six elements and their order are fixed; the version actually sent in each case is archived verbatim in the case file, and any deviation is recorded there. The invitation is the first time the case study is raised with the client: coaches are asked not to raise it informally beforehand, and not to discuss the engagement's substance with the client between the invitation and the interview. Approach wording is treated as part of the method, since how a participant is approached shapes what they report. The complete wording, and the function of each element within it, is set out in section 12.

4.3Interview

A single recorded semi-structured interview of approximately 60 minutes, conducted with the client alone; the coach is not present. A participant's total time commitment across the whole case, including review of the draft, is approximately 90 minutes, and the invitation states this figure. The interview sequence is fixed to limit recall and demand biases:

  1. Consent confirmation and accuracy framing (accuracy is explicitly requested over positivity).
  2. Timeline reconstruction — facts and dates only, prior to any evaluation.
  3. Open elicitation — the client states the most significant change in their own words, before any prompting (Most Significant Change technique; Davies & Dart, 2005).
  4. Narrative account — context, stakes, decision, the work, reported changes.
  5. Claim probes — every headline claim is probed for a specific example and for observations reported by third parties (board, team, family).
  6. Structured measurement block (section 5).
  7. Negative elicitation — three fixed questions, never omitted: what did not change; what the coach should have done differently; what might have improved without the coaching. The answers are published.

The full block-by-block structure, with time budgets and verbatim questions, is set out in section 13.

4.4Corroboration

With the client's explicit consent, one named third party may be asked a brief standardized question to confirm a key reported change. Disconfirming replies are retained and reported; they downgrade the claim's evidence tier.

5

Measurement

#

Structured measurement uses the retrospective pre-post ("then-test") design (Howard et al., 1979): for 5–8 dimensions tied to the engagement's goals, the client rates their current standing, then their standing at the reference point, as judged from their present perspective. For a completed engagement the reference point is the period immediately before the coaching began; for an ongoing engagement it is the named, dated anchor fixed under section 3, which is stated to the client rather than left to inference.

The block also records goal attainment against the goals stated in the impact model, three working-relationship items, and a single global transition rating.

All ratings are retrospective self-ratings. They are reported as such in every story, are never presented as measured outcomes, and never appear in a story's title.

6

Evidence classification

#

Every outcome claim in every published story is classified at one of three tiers:

TierDefinitionReporting language
1Client account The client's own report "The client reports…", "attributes…"
2Reported observation Observations by others, as reported by the client "The client says her board commented…"
3Corroborated Confirmed by a document or by a second person "Confirmed by…"

Quantitative claims that cannot be corroborated are either attributed to the client as their own estimate or omitted. The designation "verified" means precisely: a real person and a real engagement, interviewed independently under this protocol, verbatim transcript on file, every word approved by the client in writing, and every claim labeled at its actual tier. It does not mean the coaching has been proven to cause the reported outcomes.

7

Claim discipline and known limitations

#

The following claims are never made:

Causation

A retrospective single-case design cannot establish causal effect. Published stories use contribution language only (Mayne, 2008; 2012): the strongest permissible claim is that the evidence is consistent with the coaching contributing to the reported change, alongside other factors. Every story includes a paragraph naming those other factors, drawn from the client's own answers.

Typicality

A selected success case supports no claim about average or expected outcomes.

Return on investment

ROI multiples in the coaching industry rest on self-estimated figures that do not withstand methodological scrutiny (Grant, 2012; De Meuse et al., 2009). No ROI figures are published.

Two residual risks are acknowledged rather than claimed away.

Residual risk 1 — the interviewer is not blind

The impact model is built with the coach and known to the interviewer before the interview. The protocol contains this through fixed sequencing (open elicitation before any prompting), verbatim scripts, quarantined predictions, and published negative answers; it does not eliminate it.

Residual risk 2 — corroborators are client-nominated

Where a third party corroborates a claim, that person is nominated by the client. Corroboration is therefore a bounded check by someone positioned to observe the change, not independent verification by a disinterested party, and disconfirming replies are retained precisely because the check would otherwise carry little weight.

For context: meta-analytic evidence finds moderate positive effects of workplace coaching in rigorous trials (Theeboom et al., 2014; de Haan & Nilsson, 2023), and self-reported change tends to exceed observer-rated change. The Institute's stories are self-reports, systematically taken and labeled accordingly; each story carries an identical limitations note stating this.

8

Publication criteria

#

A story is declined — not reshaped — when any of the following holds: the client will not be interviewed independently; headline outcomes cannot be corroborated and the client will not have them published as their own attributed account; the coach requires edits that change meaning; anonymity cannot genuinely protect the client after generalizing identifying details; the account remains internally inconsistent after follow-up; the client withdraws the negative-elicitation answers or the other-factors paragraph at review; or anything of value proves to be conditioned on a favorable account. The criteria are stated in operational form in section 17.

9

Consent, data protection, and participant rights

#

Consent is taken in two stages: before the interview (participation, recording, transcription, drafting), and on the final text (publication, final visibility level, media rights). Nothing is published without the second signature. Both forms are reproduced in full in section 15.

Participants may decline any question, withdraw before publication without explanation, and withdraw consent after publication, in which case the story is removed from the Institute's properties and the coach's. Participants choose among three visibility levels — full name, role and sector only, or fully anonymous — the latter two subject to an identifiability check conducted with the participant.

The recording is accessed only by the Institute and deleted once the final story is delivered; media excerpts approved at the second consent stage are retained. The verbatim transcript is retained as the story's audit trail and anonymized at the participant's request. Deleting the recording is a deliberate trade-off: it removes the possibility of re-auditing the transcript against the original audio, and accepts that cost in favor of participant privacy. The retained transcript is verbatim, and the client's written approval of the final text serves as the member check on its use. Transcription processing is disclosed in the consent form. Data handling follows GDPR Articles 6, 7, and 17.

10

Corrections and accountability

#

Disputes concerning any published story may be addressed to sebastien@syntoria.co. Every challenge receives a response; corrections are made visibly, with a dated note on the story. Each published story carries a methods box stating the interview date, the evidence-tier legend, the client's written approval, the disclosed selection, the fixed-fee condition, and a link to this methodology.

11

Methodological basis

#

The protocol integrates: the Success Case Method (Brinkerhoff, 2003; 2005) for disclosed best-case selection and corroboration standards; contribution analysis (Mayne, 2008; 2012) for permissible causal language; qualitative trustworthiness criteria (Lincoln & Guba, 1985; Yin, 2018) for member checking, audit trail, and rival explanations; retrospective pre-post measurement (Howard et al., 1979) for structured ratings; and FTC endorsement regulation and the ICC/ESOMAR code as the compliance floor for commissioned testimonials.

This methodology is currently undergoing external methodological review. Full references are given in Appendix A.

Part II

The full protocol

Part I states what we do. This part shows the instruments themselves — the exact wording, the interview structure, the claim rules — so that a reader can check that the method is fixed rather than improvised. Scripts are reproduced verbatim; where they appear in quotation marks, the first person is the interviewer or the coach speaking as they actually speak.

12

The invitation — exact wording

#

This email is the first time the case study is put to the client. Coaches are asked not to raise it informally beforehand, and not to discuss the engagement's substance with the client between the invitation and the interview; where either has happened, it is recorded in the case file as a deviation. In the first case, the coach had raised the study with the client personally before this rule was fixed; that case's file records it, along with the version of the email actually sent.

The wording below is the current standard. Its six beats and their order are fixed; the version sent in each case is archived verbatim in the case file, and material changes to the standard wording are versioned in this method. It is sent by the coach, as plain text, with no attachment.

We publish it because how a participant is approached shapes what they say — and because it establishes, before the client has said anything, that the coach steps out of the process and that nothing reaches them until the client has approved it.

SubjectCoaching Case Study

Hi [client first name],

I have a favor to ask.

I'm having an independent case study produced about my coaching work, and ours is the engagement I'd most like it to be about.

It's run by Sébastien Tomàs of The Syntoria Institute. Everything is on this page: what it involves, how much of your time it takes (about an hour and a half in total), and the short form to pick a time that suits you:

[client-facing page URL — one per case]

One thing worth knowing: Sébastien interviews us separately, and nothing reaches me until you've approved it. So I'm stepping out from here. The page has his contact for anything you need.

And if you look at it and it isn't for you, no hard feelings at all.

Thanks, [client first name]. This means a lot to me.

Warmly,
[coach first name]

What each beat is doing

BeatElementMethodological function
1The ask, carried by the relationshipThe warrant is the coach's existing relationship, never the Institute's credibility. The Institute does not recruit participants; the coach asks, once, in their own name.
2Who runs it, one lineEnough to place the Institute, deliberately not enough to become a pitch. A persuasive frame here would contaminate everything that follows.
3The page carries everything, time cost stated up frontFull disclosure is moved to a page the participant reads alone, at their own pace, with no reply owed to anyone.
4Independence stated plainly; the coach visibly steps out"Nothing reaches me until you've approved it" states the structural independence in the participant's own language rather than in methodological terms.
5The easy noAn explicit, cost-free exit protects the candor of everything said later. A participant who could not refuse cannot be relied on to disagree.
6The personal thank-youThe ask remains personal. The relationship carries the request; the Institute never does.
What is deliberately absent

No methodology explanation (it is on the page) · no visibility levels or consent detail (page and form checkboxes) · no attachment · no second link · no request to reply. The client's only action is page → form → booking. Stage-A consent is completed on the form, before any interview slot exists; the form leads to the booking page and never the reverse.

What the page states

The client-facing page linked from the email carries, in fixed sections: who The Syntoria Institute is and who conducts the interview; what the case study is and how the coach will use it; the client's control — approval of every word, the three visibility levels, the right to withdraw; the total time commitment; how the interview is recorded and what happens to the recording and transcript; and the Institute's direct contact. The page ends at the stage-A consent form, which leads to the booking page — never the reverse. The page is built from a fixed template, personalized only in named fields; template copy is available on request.

The interview, block by block

#

Order matters. Facts before evaluation, to limit recall bias. The open question before any prompting, to prevent leading. Ratings only after the narrative, so that numbers describe the account rather than replace it.

The budgets below sum to approximately 58 minutes within a 60-minute interview; the slack is deliberate. Where a conversation runs long, the optional corroboration request in block 8 is the first item dropped and the measurement block absorbs the trim. Negative elicitation is never cut.

BlockStageTimeWhat happens
1Consent recap + accuracy frame2 minConsent reconfirmed on the recording, then the accuracy framing line below.
2Timeline reconstruction7 minFacts and dates only — role, context, start, turning points. No evaluation yet.
3Open elicitation4 minOne question, before any prompting. Their words only, then a single "why that one?"
4The narrative22 minContext → what was at stake → the decision, including the hesitation → the work → what changed.
5Claim probeswoven inPer headline claim, two mandatory probes: a specific recent example, and what others actually noticed.
6Structured measurement10 minThen-test ratings, goal attainment against the impact model, three working-relationship items, one global change rating.
7Negative elicitation7 minThree questions, never skipped, answers published.
8Optional corroboration2 minIf a strong claim surfaced: permission to send one named third party a two-line confirmation question.
9Wrap4 minAnything off the record, provisional visibility preference, next steps.
Block 1 — the accuracy frame, verbatim

"Before we start: you've signed the consent form — I just want to confirm on the recording that you're okay with this conversation being recorded and used to produce a written story that [coach] will use publicly, and that you'll approve every word before anyone sees it. … One more thing, and it's the most important thing I'll say: the value of this whole exercise depends on accuracy, not positivity. If something didn't work, was slow, or is overstated, saying so makes the story more credible, not less. I will ask you directly about what didn't change."

Block 3 — the open elicitation, verbatim

"Looking at the whole engagement — in your view, what was the most significant change that came out of it? Take your time."

Nothing may precede this question except blocks 1 and 2. No dimension, goal, or expected outcome is named to the client before they have answered it.

Block 5 — the two mandatory probes

Example: "Give me a specific recent example of you doing that."

External echo: "Did anyone notice — your team, your board, at home? What exactly did they say?"

A claim with neither an example nor an echo is published only as a Tier-1 self-report, or not at all.

Block 7 — negative elicitation, verbatim

"What didn't change — what did you hope would move that didn't?"

"If [coach] were listening, what would you tell them to do differently?"

"Honest question: what might have improved anyway during that period, even without the coaching — new role, market, team changes, your own drive?"

The third answer becomes the story's other factors paragraph, published in every story. It is the client's own account of rival explanations, gathered before the writing begins rather than constructed afterwards.

Block 8 — the third-party confirmation request

"You mentioned [claim]. Would you be comfortable with me sending [named person] a two-line email asking whether they observed that too? Two lines, one reply, nothing more."

The email sent is itself standardized, and explicitly invites a negative answer:

SubjectTwo-line question about [client first name]

"Hello [name], I'm writing a short case study about [client]'s work with their executive coach — with [client]'s full consent, and they suggested you. One question, two lines is plenty: did you notice a change in [the specific claim] over [period]? Whatever you saw — including 'no change' — is useful. Thank you! — Sébastien Tomàs, The Syntoria Institute"

Replies are logged verbatim in the case file. "No change" answers are kept and honored: they downgrade the claim's tier, which is the mechanism working as designed.

14

The retrospective ratings

#

For each case we select 5–8 dimensions tied to the engagement's goals, drawn from a fixed menu:

  • Decision-making under pressure
  • Delegation
  • Difficult conversations
  • Board and stakeholder relations
  • Team climate as the client experiences it
  • Clarity of priorities
  • Confidence in role
  • Stress and energy
  • Listening
  • Strategic versus operational balance

The script is always the same, and the order is always NOW first, THEN second:

The then-test script, verbatim

"Where are you now on [dimension], 1 to 10? … And looking back from where you sit today — where were you at [the reference point]?"

The block is announced to the client rather than slipped in:

Announcing the block, verbatim

"I'm going to ask you a short series of structured questions now — same ones for every person I interview. Some will feel like survey questions; that's deliberate, it's what makes stories comparable and honest."

The remainder of the measurement block, verbatim

Goal attainment

Each engagement goal from the impact model is read back:

"On this goal — would you say the work fell short of, met, or exceeded what you expected? … What tells you that?"

Working relationship — three items, rated 1 to 5

"We agreed on what I needed to work on."

"The way we worked together suited me."

"I felt understood."

Global transition rating — one item, on a fixed seven-point scale

"Compared to before the coaching, how would you rate the overall change in how you operate as a leader — much worse, worse, slightly worse, no change, slightly better, better, much better?"

15

Consent — the two stages in full

#
Stage A

Before the interview — participation and recording

  • What this is: a ~60-minute recorded conversation about the client's experience of working with [coach], conducted independently by the Institute — the coach is not present. Total time across the whole case is approximately an hour and a half, including reviewing the draft.
  • What it becomes: a written case study that [coach] will use publicly (website, proposals, social). The Institute is engaged by [coach] under a fixed arrangement that does not depend on what the client says.
  • Separate consents, ticked individually: record the conversation · transcribe it (the transcription service is named; a fully on-device tool is offered on request) · use the client's words to draft a story for their approval · archive recording and transcript in the case file.
  • Data: recording and transcript seen only by the Institute, stored privately in the EU or under EU-approved safeguards. The recording is deleted once the final story is delivered; video excerpts approved at stage B are retained. The verbatim transcript is kept as the story's audit trail, and anonymized on request.
  • Rights: participation is voluntary; the client may stop the interview, decline any question, or withdraw entirely at any time before publication, without explanation. After publication they may withdraw consent, and the story is removed from the Institute's properties and the coach's — with the honest caveat that copies already circulated by third parties may be beyond reach.
  • Nothing is published without stage B.
Stage B

On the final text — publication

  • The client confirms they have read the final version, dated, and approve every word.
  • Final visibility: full name · role and sector only · fully anonymous. The latter two are confirmed only after an identifiability check conducted with the client.
  • Media rights: text · optionally audio excerpts · optionally video excerpts.
  • Withdrawal contact and retention terms restated.

Approval order — fixed and client-protective

  1. ClientThe client reviews draft 1 and may correct or remove anything, without explanation. One consequence is fixed in advance: a story that loses its negative-elicitation answers or its other-factors paragraph at this step is declined under section 17, never published without them.
  2. ClientThe client authorizes draft 2 being shared with the coach.
  3. CoachThe coach reviews, scoped to factual errors and confidentiality only. The coach cannot soften the friction, upgrade a claim's tier, or add outcomes. Every coach-requested change and its resolution is logged in the case file.
  4. ClientThe client validates last and signs stage-B consent on the final text.

Claim language — allowed and forbidden

#
AllowedForbidden
"The client reports…" / "She attributes X to the coaching" "The coaching caused / delivered / produced X" (unqualified)
"Client-rated change, looking back: 3 → 8 (retrospective self-rating)" Any rating presented as a measured outcome
"He says his board noticed…" (Tier 2) Presenting a Tier-2 claim as an observed fact
"Confirmed by [document / named role]" — Tier 3 only, when the check was actually done "Verified" on anything that did not pass a corroboration check
"The evidence is consistent with the coaching contributing to X, alongside [other factors]" Any ROI multiple or percentage
"This case shows what this engagement produced for this client" "Clients achieve…" / "results like these" / any typicality implication
The client's own words, carried from spoken to written form (fillers, grammar, spoken syntax), meaning untouched, final wording approved by the client in writing Quotes that change or add meaning; merged or composite quotes
The limitations note — identical and verbatim in every published story

What this story is and isn't.

This is a single, retrospective case, deliberately selected as a success story — it shows what this coaching produced for this client, not what every client experiences. Outcomes are the client's own account, gathered after the engagement ended; research shows self-reported change tends to read stronger than observer-rated change. Ratings are retrospective self-ratings: they measure experienced change from the client's current perspective, not objective measurement. Where a claim was corroborated by a document or another person, we say so; where it wasn't, it remains the client's account. Other factors named in the story also contributed — we do not claim the coaching alone caused these results.

17

When a story is declined

#

A story is declined — not reshaped — when any of the following holds:

  1. The client will not be interviewed independently, or the coach insists on being present.
  2. Headline outcomes cannot be corroborated and the client will not have them published as their own attributed account.
  3. The coach demands edits that change meaning, soften the friction, or upgrade a claim.
  4. Anonymity cannot survive the identifiability check even after generalizing details.
  5. The account is internally inconsistent and remains so after a follow-up.
  6. The client withdraws their negative-elicitation answers or the other-factors paragraph at review. Those elements are load-bearing; a story without them would no longer be a product of this method.
  7. Anything of value turns out to be conditioned on a positive account — a hard stop, and everyone involved is told why.

Declining is part of the product, not a failure of it. One row is logged per candidate story — proposed, interviewed, published, or declined with a reason category — and from a sufficient number of completed cases onward, the declined proportion is reported publicly.
